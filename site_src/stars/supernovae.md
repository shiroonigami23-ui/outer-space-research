A supernova can briefly outshine its whole galaxy, releasing as much energy as the Sun will in its entire life.

## Two main types

| Type | Cause | Leaves behind | Use |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Core-collapse (II, Ib, Ic)** | Iron core of a >8-Sun star collapses | Neutron star or black hole | Forges and spreads oxygen, silicon, calcium |
| **Thermonuclear (Ia)** | White dwarf pushed over ~1.4 Suns ignites | Nothing | 'Standard candles' that revealed dark energy |

## Supernovae in history

- **1006 CE**: the brightest stellar event ever recorded, seen worldwide.
- **1054 CE**: recorded by Chinese astronomers; its remnant is the [Crab Nebula](/wiki/galaxies/crab-nebula-m1.html).
- **1572**: Tycho Brahe's supernova showed that the heavens change.
- **1604**: Kepler's supernova, the last seen in the Milky Way.
- **1987**: **SN 1987A** in the Large Magellanic Cloud. Two dozen neutrinos detected hours before the light confirmed the core-collapse theory, and JWST found its neutron star in 2024.
- **2023**: SN 2023ixf in the Pinwheel Galaxy, the closest in a decade.

## Next nearby candidates

**Betelgeuse** (550 light-years) will explode sometime in the next ~100,000 years and be visible in daylight, but it is too far to harm Earth. A supernova would need to be within ~50 light-years to damage the ozone layer.

## Why they matter

- They spread the elements needed for planets and life.
- Shock waves trigger new star formation (possibly including the Sun's).
- Iron-60 in deep-sea sediments shows nearby supernovae showered Earth ~2–3 million years ago.
