| Method | How it works | Best at | Share of discoveries |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Transit** | A planet crossing its star dims it slightly (Earth dims the Sun by 0.008%) | Short-period planets; gives radius and atmosphere via spectroscopy | ~75% |
| **Radial velocity** | The star wobbles, shifting its spectrum (Doppler effect) | Massive planets near their stars; gives minimum mass | ~18% |
| **Microlensing** | A foreground star and planet bend a background star's light | Cold planets far from their stars; rogue planets | ~4% |
| **Direct imaging** | Block the starlight with a coronagraph and photograph the planet | Young, hot giants on wide orbits | ~1% |
| **Timing** | Pulsar or eclipse timing variations | Pulsar planets | <1% |
| **Astrometry** | The star's tiny sideways motion | Gaia's long-period giants | Growing |

## Key missions

Kepler (2009–18), TESS (2018–), JWST (atmospheres), CHEOPS, Gaia (astrometry), Roman Space Telescope (launched 2026, microlensing survey), PLATO (ESA, ~2026–27), and the proposed Habitable Worlds Observatory (2040s) to image Earth twins.

## Reading an atmosphere

When starlight passes through a planet's atmosphere during transit, molecules absorb particular colours. JWST has detected water, CO₂, methane, sulfur dioxide and more, and is testing claims like dimethyl sulfide on [K2-18 b](/wiki/library/k2-18b-debate.html).
