| Type | What it is | Size | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Yellow dwarf** | An ordinary G-type main-sequence star | ~1 Sun | The Sun |
| **Orange dwarf** | K-type main-sequence star; long-lived and calm, possibly the best hosts for life | 0.5–0.8 Sun | Alpha Centauri B |
| **Red dwarf** | M-type main-sequence star; ~75% of all stars | 0.08–0.5 Sun | Proxima Centauri, TRAPPIST-1 |
| **Brown dwarf** | A 'failed star' too small to fuse ordinary hydrogen | ~Jupiter-sized | Gliese 229B, WISE 0855 |
| **White dwarf** | Dead core of a Sun-like star | Earth-sized | Sirius B |
| **Black dwarf** | A fully cooled white dwarf | Earth-sized | None yet |
| **Blue dwarf** | A hypothetical late stage of red dwarfs | — | None yet |

## Red dwarfs and life

Red dwarfs are so common that most Earth-sized planets in habitable zones probably orbit them. But they flare violently when young and planets close to them may be tidally locked, with one side always facing the star. JWST is testing whether planets like [TRAPPIST-1e](/wiki/exoplanets/trappist-1e.html) can keep atmospheres.

## Brown dwarfs

Between ~13 and ~80 Jupiter masses. They glow from heat left over from formation and cool for ever. The coldest, **WISE 0855−0714**, is below the freezing point of water and has water-ice clouds. Some free-floating 'rogue planets' blur the line between planets and brown dwarfs.
