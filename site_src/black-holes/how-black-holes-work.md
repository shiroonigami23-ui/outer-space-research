A black hole is a region of space where gravity is so strong that the escape velocity exceeds the speed of light.

## Anatomy of a black hole

| Part | What it is |
|---|---|
| **Singularity** | Where general relativity predicts infinite density; a sign that a quantum theory of gravity is needed. |
| **Event horizon** | The point of no return. For a non-spinning black hole its radius is the Schwarzschild radius: ~3 km per solar mass. |
| **Photon sphere** | At 1.5× the horizon radius, light can orbit the black hole. |
| **ISCO** | The innermost stable circular orbit, the inner edge of most accretion disks. |
| **Accretion disk** | Gas spiralling in, heated to millions of degrees; it can outshine whole galaxies (quasars). |
| **Jets** | Beams of particles launched near light speed by magnetic fields and spin. |

## What would happen if you fell in?

- **Tidal forces** stretch you lengthwise ([spaghettification](/wiki/objects/spaghettification.html)). Near a small black hole this kills you before the horizon; near a supermassive one you could cross the horizon intact.
- **Time dilation:** a distant observer sees you slow down and redden, never quite crossing; you cross without noticing anything special.
- Inside, all paths lead to the singularity.

## Types

| Type | Mass | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Stellar | 3–100 Suns | Cygnus X-1, [Gaia BH3](/wiki/stars/gaia-bh3.html) |
| Intermediate | 100–100,000 Suns | Omega Centauri (2024 evidence) |
| Supermassive | Millions to billions of Suns | [Sgr A*](/wiki/stars/sgr-a.html), M87* |
| Primordial | Any (hypothetical) | Not yet found |

## How we know they exist

1. **X-ray binaries** (Cygnus X-1, 1971).
2. **Stars orbiting Sgr A\***, tracked for 30 years by Reinhard Genzel's and Andrea Ghez's teams (Nobel 2020, shared with Roger Penrose).
3. **Gravitational waves** from merging black holes (since 2015). See [Gravitational waves](/wiki/black-holes/gravitational-waves.html).
4. **Event Horizon Telescope images** of M87* (2019) and Sgr A* (2022), showing the shadow ringed by light.

## Black holes are not cosmic vacuum cleaners

Replace the Sun with a black hole of the same mass and Earth's orbit would not change. Black holes only 'suck' when you get close.

## Information and Hawking radiation

Stephen Hawking showed (1974) that black holes should slowly evaporate. If they do, what happens to the information about what fell in? This **information paradox** remains a central puzzle linking gravity and quantum mechanics.
