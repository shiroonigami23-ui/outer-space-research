## The dark ages (380,000 – ~100 million years)

After the CMB was released the universe went dark. There were no stars, only cooling hydrogen and helium gas and invisible dark matter slowly clumping under gravity.

## The first stars (Population III)

Within roughly 100–200 million years, gas falling into dark-matter clumps became dense enough to ignite fusion. The first stars contained only hydrogen and helium. They are thought to have been **huge (tens to hundreds of Suns), very hot and short-lived**, dying in supernovae that forged the first heavy elements. No Population III star has yet been confirmed, though JWST is searching.

## Reionisation

Ultraviolet light from early stars and galaxies stripped electrons from the neutral hydrogen filling space, 'reionising' the universe by about 1 billion years after the Big Bang.

## JWST's surprise

:::data Record-breaking early galaxies
- **JADES-GS-z14-0**: confirmed at redshift 14.3 in 2024.
- **MoM-z14**: confirmed at **z = 14.44**, about 280 million years after the Big Bang, the current record. See [Naidu et al. 2026](/wiki/library/naidu-2026.html).
- Many early galaxies are **brighter and more numerous than models predicted**, and some host surprisingly massive black holes.
:::

These findings do not overturn the Big Bang, but they challenge how fast stars and black holes could form. Proposed explanations include more efficient star formation, a different stellar mass mix, or black holes that started heavy ('direct collapse').

## From first galaxies to today

Small galaxies merged into larger ones. The Milky Way's oldest stars are ~13 billion years old, and its disk formed later. Star formation across the universe peaked about **10 billion years ago** ('cosmic noon') and has been declining since.
