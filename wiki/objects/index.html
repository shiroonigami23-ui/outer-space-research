🕳 Cosmic Objects & Phenomena

Black holes, white holes, wormholes, neutron stars, pulsars, magnetars, supernovae, kilonovae, galaxy collisions, gravitational waves, dark matter, voids and more, with how each works and the evidence for it.

43 entries in 4 groups.

📊 Charts and statistics for this catalog
Stars & stellar types

Main-sequence star

Stars like the Sun that fuse hydrogen into helium in their cores, about 90% of a star's life.

Stars & stellar types

Red dwarf

The most common stars in the galaxy (~75%), small, cool and able to shine for trillions of years.

Stars & stellar types

Brown dwarf

'Failed stars' between ~13 and ~80 Jupiter masses that never sustain hydrogen fusion.

Stars & stellar types

Red giant

What Sun-like stars become when core hydrogen runs out: swollen to hundreds of times their size.

Stars & stellar types

Supergiant and hypergiant

The largest and most luminous stars, destined to explode.

Stars & stellar types

White dwarf

The hot, Earth-sized remnant core of a Sun-like star, supported by electron degeneracy pressure.

Stars & stellar types

Black dwarf

A white dwarf cooled until it no longer glows, which takes far longer than the universe's current age.

Stars & stellar types

Neutron star

The collapsed core of a massive star: more mass than the Sun packed into a city-sized ball ~20 km across.

Stars & stellar types

Pulsar

A spinning neutron star sweeping radio beams across Earth like a lighthouse.

Stars & stellar types

Magnetar

Neutron stars with the strongest magnetic fields in the universe (~10¹⁵ gauss).

Stars & stellar types

Variable star (Cepheid)

Pulsating stars whose period reveals their true brightness, used as cosmic distance markers.

Stars & stellar types

Wolf–Rayet star

Hot, massive stars stripping off their own outer layers in violent winds.

Explosions & collisions

Supernova (core-collapse)

The explosion of a massive star whose iron core collapses in under a second.

Explosions & collisions

Type Ia supernova

A white dwarf exploding after gaining too much mass, a 'standard candle' that revealed dark energy.

Explosions & collisions

Kilonova

The explosion when two neutron stars merge, forging gold, platinum and other heavy elements.

Explosions & collisions

Gamma-ray burst

The most energetic explosions known, flashes of gamma rays lasting milliseconds to minutes.

Explosions & collisions

Nova

A white dwarf flaring as hydrogen from a companion ignites on its surface.

Explosions & collisions

Stellar collision

Stars actually crashing together, common only in dense star clusters.

Explosions & collisions

Galaxy collision

Galaxies merge over hundreds of millions of years; stars rarely touch but gas collapses into new stars.

Explosions & collisions

Black hole merger

Two black holes spiralling together and releasing more power than all the stars in the universe, as gravitational waves.

Explosions & collisions

Fast radio burst

Millisecond flashes of radio waves from distant galaxies.

Black holes & spacetime

Black hole

A region where gravity is so strong that nothing, not even light, can escape.

Black holes & spacetime

Stellar-mass black hole

Black holes of a few to ~100 solar masses left by dead massive stars.

Black holes & spacetime

Supermassive black hole

Monsters of millions to billions of solar masses at the centres of galaxies.

Black holes & spacetime

Intermediate-mass black hole

Missing-link black holes of ~100 to 100,000 Suns.

Black holes & spacetime

Primordial black hole

Hypothetical black holes formed in the first second after the Big Bang; a dark-matter candidate.

Black holes & spacetime

Hawking radiation

Stephen Hawking's 1974 prediction that black holes slowly evaporate by emitting radiation.

Black holes & spacetime

Accretion disk and jets

Swirling gas around black holes, heated to millions of degrees, launching jets at nearly light speed.

Black holes & spacetime

Quasar

The brightest objects in the universe: supermassive black holes feeding voraciously.

Black holes & spacetime

White hole

The time-reverse of a black hole: a region that nothing can enter but from which matter and light pour out.

Black holes & spacetime

Wormhole

A hypothetical tunnel through spacetime linking distant places or times (an Einstein–Rosen bridge).

Black holes & spacetime

Gravitational waves

Ripples in spacetime predicted by Einstein in 1916 and first detected in 2015.

Black holes & spacetime

Gravitational lensing

Massive objects bend light, magnifying and distorting background galaxies.

Black holes & spacetime

Spaghettification

Being stretched into a noodle by tidal forces near a black hole.

The universe at large

Cosmic microwave background

The afterglow of the Big Bang, light released 380,000 years after it began.

The universe at large

Dark matter

Invisible matter making up ~85% of all matter, revealed only by its gravity.

The universe at large

Dark energy

The mysterious cause of the accelerating expansion, ~68% of the universe's energy.

The universe at large

Cosmic void

Vast near-empty regions between galaxy filaments, up to hundreds of millions of light-years across.

The universe at large

The vacuum of space

Space is not perfectly empty: intergalactic space holds about one atom per cubic metre.

The universe at large

Cosmic web

The large-scale structure of the universe: filaments, walls and nodes of galaxies around voids.

The universe at large

Cosmic rays

High-energy particles from space, mostly protons, some with energies far beyond human accelerators.

The universe at large

Nebula

Clouds of gas and dust where stars are born or have died.

The universe at large

Stardust (cosmic dust)

Tiny grains of silicate and carbon forged in dying stars, the raw material of planets and of us.
