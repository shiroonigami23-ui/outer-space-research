# Stars & stellar types|⭐
Main-sequence star|Observed|Stars like the Sun that fuse hydrogen into helium in their cores, about 90% of a star's life.|Gravity squeezes the core to ~15 million K, where hydrogen fuses; outward radiation pressure balances gravity in hydrostatic equilibrium.|The Hertzsprung–Russell diagram; helioseismology of the Sun.|-|Main_sequence
Red dwarf|Observed|The most common stars in the galaxy (~75%), small, cool and able to shine for trillions of years.|Low mass (0.08–0.6 Sun) means slow fusion and fully convective interiors that mix fuel.|Proxima Centauri, TRAPPIST-1; frequent flares challenge habitability of their planets.|-|Red_dwarf
Brown dwarf|Observed|'Failed stars' between ~13 and ~80 Jupiter masses that never sustain hydrogen fusion.|Too light to fuse ordinary hydrogen; they briefly burn deuterium and then cool for ever.|First confirmed in 1995 (Gliese 229B, Teide 1); WISE found the coldest, near room temperature.|-|Brown_dwarf
Red giant|Observed|What Sun-like stars become when core hydrogen runs out: swollen to hundreds of times their size.|The core contracts and heats while a hydrogen-burning shell inflates the outer layers; later helium ignites in a 'helium flash'.|Arcturus, Aldebaran; the Sun will become one in ~5 billion years.|-|Red_giant
Supergiant and hypergiant|Observed|The largest and most luminous stars, destined to explode.|Massive stars (>8 Suns) fuse heavier elements in shells like an onion up to iron.|Betelgeuse, Rigel, UY Scuti, Eta Carinae.|-|Supergiant_star
White dwarf|Observed|The hot, Earth-sized remnant core of a Sun-like star, supported by electron degeneracy pressure.|After a red giant sheds a planetary nebula, the exposed core (mostly carbon and oxygen) slowly cools; maximum mass ~1.4 Suns (Chandrasekhar limit).|Sirius B; ~97% of all stars will end this way.|-|White_dwarf
Black dwarf|Theoretical|A white dwarf cooled until it no longer glows, which takes far longer than the universe's current age.|Cooling over ~10¹⁵ years.|None exist yet: the universe is only 13.8 billion years old.|-|Black_dwarf
Neutron star|Observed|The collapsed core of a massive star: more mass than the Sun packed into a city-sized ball ~20 km across.|In a core-collapse supernova, electrons and protons merge into neutrons; neutron degeneracy halts the collapse.|A teaspoon weighs ~a billion tonnes; NICER measures their sizes.|-|Neutron_star
Pulsar|Observed|A spinning neutron star sweeping radio beams across Earth like a lighthouse.|Strong magnetic fields channel radiation from the poles; rotation makes it pulse.|Discovered by Jocelyn Bell Burnell in 1967; millisecond pulsars spin over 700 times a second.|pulsar sound|Pulsar
Magnetar|Observed|Neutron stars with the strongest magnetic fields in the universe (~10¹⁵ gauss).|Decaying magnetic fields crack the crust, releasing giant flares; linked to some fast radio bursts.|SGR 1806-20's 2004 giant flare; SGR 1935+2154 produced a fast radio burst in 2020.|-|Magnetar
Variable star (Cepheid)|Observed|Pulsating stars whose period reveals their true brightness, used as cosmic distance markers.|Layers of ionised helium trap and release heat, driving pulsations.|Henrietta Leavitt's 1912 period–luminosity law let Hubble measure distances to galaxies.|-|Cepheid_variable
Wolf–Rayet star|Observed|Hot, massive stars stripping off their own outer layers in violent winds.|Intense radiation drives winds that expose helium-, carbon- or nitrogen-rich layers.|WR 104, WR 140 dust shells imaged by JWST.|-|Wolf%E2%80%93Rayet_star
# Explosions & collisions|💥
Supernova (core-collapse)|Observed|The explosion of a massive star whose iron core collapses in under a second.|Iron cannot release energy by fusion; the core collapses into a neutron star or black hole and the rebound plus neutrinos blow the star apart.|SN 1987A in the Large Magellanic Cloud, the first detected by neutrinos; it forged and scattered elements like oxygen and calcium.|-|Supernova
Type Ia supernova|Observed|A white dwarf exploding after gaining too much mass, a 'standard candle' that revealed dark energy.|Accretion from a companion or a white-dwarf merger triggers runaway carbon fusion.|1998 discovery of the accelerating universe (Nobel Prize 2011).|-|Type_Ia_supernova
Kilonova|Observed|The explosion when two neutron stars merge, forging gold, platinum and other heavy elements.|Rapid neutron capture (r-process) in ejected neutron-rich matter.|GW170817 (2017): gravitational waves plus light from the same event, first multi-messenger observation.|-|Kilonova
Gamma-ray burst|Observed|The most energetic explosions known, flashes of gamma rays lasting milliseconds to minutes.|Long bursts come from collapsing massive stars (collapsars); short bursts from neutron star mergers.|GRB 221009A (2022), the 'BOAT', brightest of all time.|-|Gamma-ray_burst
Nova|Observed|A white dwarf flaring as hydrogen from a companion ignites on its surface.|Thermonuclear runaway on the surface; the star survives and can repeat.|T Coronae Borealis, a recurrent nova, was expected to erupt in 2024–26.|-|Nova
Stellar collision|Observed (rare)|Stars actually crashing together, common only in dense star clusters.|In globular cluster cores, close encounters merge stars into 'blue stragglers'.|V1309 Scorpii (2008) was seen merging as a 'red nova'.|-|Stellar_collision
Galaxy collision|Observed|Galaxies merge over hundreds of millions of years; stars rarely touch but gas collapses into new stars.|Gravity pulls galaxies together; tidal forces fling out streams of stars.|The Antennae galaxies; the Milky Way and Andromeda will merge in ~4–5 billion years (a 2025 study put the odds near 50%).|-|Galaxy_merger
Black hole merger|Observed|Two black holes spiralling together and releasing more power than all the stars in the universe, as gravitational waves.|Orbital energy is radiated as ripples in spacetime until the horizons merge and 'ring down'.|GW150914 (first detection, 2015); GW250114 confirmed Hawking's area law at ~99.999% confidence.|gravitational wave chirp|Binary_black_hole
Fast radio burst|Observed|Millisecond flashes of radio waves from distant galaxies.|Many likely come from magnetars.|First found in 2007; CHIME detects them daily; FRB 20200428 came from a Milky Way magnetar.|-|Fast_radio_burst
# Black holes & spacetime|🕳
Black hole|Observed|A region where gravity is so strong that nothing, not even light, can escape.|Forms when matter collapses within its Schwarzschild radius; the boundary is the event horizon; general relativity predicts a singularity inside.|X-ray binaries (Cygnus X-1), stars orbiting Sgr A* (Nobel 2020), gravitational waves, Event Horizon Telescope images (M87* 2019, Sgr A* 2022).|black hole sonification Perseus|Black_hole
Stellar-mass black hole|Observed|Black holes of a few to ~100 solar masses left by dead massive stars.|Core collapse of stars above ~20–25 Suns.|Gaia BH3 (33 Suns), the most massive in the Milky Way.|-|Stellar_black_hole
Supermassive black hole|Observed|Monsters of millions to billions of solar masses at the centres of galaxies.|Grow by accreting gas and merging; how the first ones grew so fast is a major puzzle.|Sgr A* (4.3 million Suns); M87* (6.5 billion); JWST finds them less than 500 million years after the Big Bang.|black hole sonification M87|Supermassive_black_hole
Intermediate-mass black hole|Observed (few)|Missing-link black holes of ~100 to 100,000 Suns.|May form in dense clusters by runaway mergers.|Fast-moving stars in Omega Centauri (2024) revealed one of at least ~8,200 Suns; GW190521 produced a ~142-Sun remnant.|-|Intermediate-mass_black_hole
Primordial black hole|Theoretical|Hypothetical black holes formed in the first second after the Big Bang; a dark-matter candidate.|Dense fluctuations in the early universe could collapse directly.|No confirmed detection; microlensing surveys constrain them.|-|Primordial_black_hole
Hawking radiation|Theoretical|Stephen Hawking's 1974 prediction that black holes slowly evaporate by emitting radiation.|Quantum effects near the horizon cause a faint thermal glow; tiny black holes evaporate fastest.|Not observed directly (far too faint); analogues seen in lab experiments.|-|Hawking_radiation
Accretion disk and jets|Observed|Swirling gas around black holes, heated to millions of degrees, launching jets at nearly light speed.|Friction and magnetic fields heat infalling matter; spin and magnetic fields launch jets.|M87's 5,000-light-year jet; quasars outshine entire galaxies.|-|Accretion_disk
Quasar|Observed|The brightest objects in the universe: supermassive black holes feeding voraciously.|Gas falling into a black hole radiates intensely before crossing the horizon.|3C 273 identified in 1963; the most distant seen at z>7.|-|Quasar
White hole|Theoretical|The time-reverse of a black hole: a region that nothing can enter but from which matter and light pour out.|A valid mathematical solution of Einstein's equations (the extended Schwarzschild spacetime), but unstable; some speculate black holes may end as white holes or that the Big Bang was one.|No observational evidence.|-|White_hole
Wormhole|Theoretical|A hypothetical tunnel through spacetime linking distant places or times (an Einstein–Rosen bridge).|Allowed by general relativity, but keeping one open would need 'exotic matter' with negative energy; quantum effects may forbid it.|No evidence; studied in theory and quantum-gravity 'ER = EPR' ideas.|-|Wormhole
Gravitational waves|Observed|Ripples in spacetime predicted by Einstein in 1916 and first detected in 2015.|Accelerating masses, especially merging black holes and neutron stars, stretch and squeeze space as waves travel at light speed.|LIGO–Virgo–KAGRA have detected ~300 events; pulsar timing arrays found a background hum (2023). Nobel Prize 2017.|gravitational wave chirp|Gravitational_wave
Gravitational lensing|Observed|Massive objects bend light, magnifying and distorting background galaxies.|Mass curves spacetime, so light follows curved paths.|Eddington's 1919 eclipse confirmed general relativity; lensing magnified Earendel.|-|Gravitational_lens
Spaghettification|Theoretical (tidal disruption observed)|Being stretched into a noodle by tidal forces near a black hole.|Gravity pulls harder on the near end of an object than the far end.|Tidal disruption events show stars shredded by black holes.|-|Spaghettification
# The universe at large|🌌
Cosmic microwave background|Observed|The afterglow of the Big Bang, light released 380,000 years after it began.|When the universe cooled to ~3,000 K, atoms formed and light travelled freely; expansion stretched it to microwaves (2.7 K).|Discovered by Penzias and Wilson (1965); mapped by COBE, WMAP and Planck.|cosmic microwave background sonification|Cosmic_microwave_background
Dark matter|Inferred|Invisible matter making up ~85% of all matter, revealed only by its gravity.|Unknown particles (or other forms) that do not interact with light.|Galaxy rotation curves (Vera Rubin), the Bullet Cluster, CMB patterns, lensing.|-|Dark_matter
Dark energy|Inferred|The mysterious cause of the accelerating expansion, ~68% of the universe's energy.|Possibly a cosmological constant; DESI (2025) hinted it may evolve over time.|Supernova surveys (1998), BAO, CMB.|-|Dark_energy
Cosmic void|Observed|Vast near-empty regions between galaxy filaments, up to hundreds of millions of light-years across.|Matter flowed out of underdense regions into filaments and clusters.|The Boötes Void (~330 million light-years across); the KBC void may surround the Milky Way.|-|Void_(astronomy)
The vacuum of space|Observed|Space is not perfectly empty: intergalactic space holds about one atom per cubic metre.|Even 'empty' space has the CMB, neutrinos, magnetic fields and quantum vacuum fluctuations.|Interstellar medium ~1 atom/cm³; Casimir effect shows vacuum energy.|-|Outer_space
Cosmic web|Observed|The large-scale structure of the universe: filaments, walls and nodes of galaxies around voids.|Dark matter clumps grew from tiny fluctuations, pulling galaxies into a web.|Galaxy redshift surveys (SDSS, DESI); filaments imaged directly in 2023–24.|-|Observable_universe
Cosmic rays|Observed|High-energy particles from space, mostly protons, some with energies far beyond human accelerators.|Accelerated by supernova shocks and active galaxies.|The 'Oh-My-God' particle (1991) and 'Amaterasu' (2023).|-|Cosmic_ray
Nebula|Observed|Clouds of gas and dust where stars are born or have died.|Emission nebulae glow from ionised gas; dark nebulae block light; planetary nebulae and supernova remnants are stellar deaths.|Orion Nebula, Pillars of Creation, Crab Nebula.|-|Nebula
Stardust (cosmic dust)|Observed|Tiny grains of silicate and carbon forged in dying stars, the raw material of planets and of us.|Condense in cool outflows of giant stars and supernova ejecta.|Presolar grains in meteorites; Stardust mission returned comet dust (2006); interstellar dust collected.|-|Cosmic_dust
